System Design for Data Engineers · Chapter 13

Reliability & Operations

Uptime tells you the box is on. It tells you nothing about whether the data inside is actually right. Real reliability means watching three separate things, not one.

Printed as "Chapter Twelve" in the book's own running heads — see the numbering note in Chapter 3. This guide cites it as Chapter 13.
The Simple Version, First

Two security guards watch the same building overnight. One only checks "is the front door locked?" The other also walks the halls checking nothing's missing, and checks the safe's contents against the inventory sheet.

Both can honestly say "everything looked fine." Only one would actually notice if something valuable quietly went missing while the doors stayed locked the whole time. A pipeline can look perfectly healthy — every job green — while quietly losing or corrupting data for months.

Why "Everything's Green" Can Still Mean Something Is Badly Wrong

Two data platforms at similar-sized payment companies run side by side. Both use the same tools. Both have on-call rotations.

Team A's dashboard shows 99.98% uptime. Quiet rotation. Leadership happy. Team B's dashboard shows that same uptime, plus freshness, completeness, and accuracy numbers — and got paged five times this quarter across the three.

Six months in, finance notices monthly revenue doesn't match reality. Team A's pipeline had silently lost 4% of events for three months. Nothing crashed, so nothing alerted. Team B caught the same class of bug in its second week, because completeness fired the moment row counts diverged.

  • Freshness — did the data arrive on time? Measured per data slice.

🚩 FAANG Signal

When you say "SLO" in a data engineering interview, the interviewer's next question is almost always "which three?" The answer is freshness, completeness, accuracy — with a specific numeric threshold for each. Not "we have SLOs." The specific three, with specific numbers.

Three ways to check completeness, cheap to expensive

ApproachWhat it doesCostUse it for
Aggregate count + totalCompares one row count and one sum on each sideCheapestCatching a whole missing day or broken filter
Sampled row-level matchJoins a small sample (~1%) by ID, compares every fieldModerateRow-level mismatches aggregates hide
Full row-level comparisonEvery row on one side checked against every row on the otherMost expensiveRegulated data where any mismatch matters

-- Three SLOs in one query, run daily on the previous day's data.
WITH partition_timing AS (
    SELECT event_date, MAX(ingested_at) AS last_ingest_ts
    FROM warehouse.revenue_events
    WHERE event_date = CURRENT_DATE - INTERVAL '1' DAY
    GROUP BY event_date
),
row_counts AS (
    SELECT
        (SELECT COUNT(*) FROM warehouse.revenue_events
         WHERE event_date = CURRENT_DATE - INTERVAL '1' DAY) AS target_rows,
        (SELECT COUNT(*) FROM source.revenue_events_mirror
         WHERE event_date = CURRENT_DATE - INTERVAL '1' DAY) AS source_rows
)
SELECT
    ROUND(100.0 * rc.target_rows / NULLIF(rc.source_rows, 0), 3) AS completeness_pct
FROM row_counts rc;

Idea 2: A Good Runbook Is Written for the Person Who's Never Seen This Before

A runbook needs five things: what the alert means (one sentence), the first three checks with exact commands, common causes with fixes, a named escalation with a time limit, and a pre-written message to send to stakeholders.

❌ Anti-Pattern

A "runbook" whose only instruction is "page the owner." That's not a runbook — it's a phone number. The point is that someone who isn't the owner can still resolve the issue using it.

✅ Say this out loud

"A good on-call runbook is written for someone who doesn't know why this DAG exists. If the runbook says 'page the owner,' it isn't a runbook."

Idea 3: Most Data Incidents Are About Drift, Not Something Breaking

A post-mortem for a data incident should be a drift analysis: what actually drifted, why wasn't it detected the moment it started, and what closes that detection gap.

⚠️ War Story

During a quarterly compliance audit, finance noticed monthly revenue in the warehouse differed from real transaction totals by about 4%. A producer-side bug had been silently dropping events for one merchant category for three months — a filter condition changed during an unrelated refactor. The only alerting that existed was uptime-based; there was no reconciliation or accuracy check at all. The fix: a three-SLO dashboard across every revenue-critical pipeline, plus a quarterly accuracy audit. Six months later, a similar bug introduced a 0.3% discrepancy — the accuracy check fired within 24 hours. Total exposure was one day instead of three months. The investment paid for itself roughly 90 times over from that one incident alone.

✅ Say this out loud

"Post-mortems for DE incidents are usually about drift, not failure. The dimension I designed around moved, and the system noticed three weeks late."

Idea 4: Practice Breaking Things on Purpose, Before Production Does It for You

Two moves, done deliberately on a schedule: inject bad data on purpose (malformed fields, out-of-range values, wrong types) and delay an upstream dependency on purpose (30 min, 1h, 2h). These two catch roughly 80% of the failure modes a data team will actually hit in production.

❌ Anti-Pattern

Borrowing chaos-engineering tools built for web services (randomly killing servers) and pointing them at a data pipeline. A data pipeline's real failure modes are bad data and slow upstreams — not crashed processes.

A Real Interview, Walked Through Simply

The classic reliability prompt: a business-critical pipeline, a recovery-time target, a team size. Watch the candidate name the three SLOs with real numbers and budget the recovery time.

Interviewer

Daily revenue roll-up feeding finance dashboards and the quarterly auditor's report. Uptime monitoring only, no runbook. Six engineers rotating weekly, two senior, four mid-level.

Candidate

High-stakes finance and audit means accuracy matters more than freshness within reasonable bounds — and the runbook has to work for a mid-level engineer on a Sunday, not just the person who built it.

Three SLOs: freshness within 30 min 99.5% of the time, completeness within 0.1% of source reconciled daily, accuracy variance under 0.1% investigated within 4 hours (paged if over 1%). Recovery budget: 15 min detect, 30 min diagnose, 45 min rerun, 30 min reconcile.

Interviewer

What breaks first?

Candidate

Reconciliation cost — a full exact count against the live system every morning adds real production load. I'd sample: 1% exact row match plus a full aggregate count and total on top. Cuts cost by two orders of magnitude, at maybe 10% less detection sensitivity.

(pauses) Actually — for a pipeline feeding the quarterly auditor's report specifically, 10% sensitivity loss isn't acceptable. Let me restructure: daily aggregate (cheap), weekly 1% sample (moderate), quarterly full comparison timed to the close window (expensive, but auditors expect it, so that load is pre-approved). Three tiers mapped to the actual regulatory calendar instead of spread evenly across the year.

Common Mistakes People Make

  1. Treating uptime as the whole reliability story. Green uptime with silent data loss is the default failure mode of a platform that hasn't gone through this exercise.
  2. Tracking data quality as one single number. The three tracks fail independently — each needs its own alert.
  3. A runbook that just says "page the owner." Name the escalation path, exact commands, likely causes, and a message template.
  4. Writing post-mortems as failure timelines instead of drift analysis. Most incidents are about a slow drift and a detection gap, not a crash.
  5. Never practicing failure on purpose. The failure mode production hits is usually the one nobody rehearsed.
Cheat Sheet
Built to be photographed before your interview.

Three Data SLOs

  • Freshness — 99.5% within 30 min of expected time
  • Completeness — target rows within 0.1% of source
  • Accuracy — variance under 0.1% vs. cross-validation

Reconciliation Tiers

  1. Aggregate count + sum — daily, cheap
  2. Sampled row-level (~1%) — weekly, moderate
  3. Full row-level diff — quarterly/regulatory, expensive

MTTR Budget Equation

recovery time = detection + diagnosis + rerun + reconcile

For a 2-hour target: 15 + 30 + 45 + 30 = 120 min.

Runbook Template

  1. Alert meaning, one sentence
  2. First three checks, with commands
  3. Common causes with fixes
  4. Named escalation, time limit
  5. Pre-written stakeholder message

Post-Mortem as Drift Analysis

  1. What drifted?
  2. Why wasn't it detected sooner?
  3. What closes the detection gap?

Three Lines Worth Memorizing

  • "Freshness, completeness, accuracy. Three SLOs, three alert routes."
  • "Write runbooks for someone who doesn't know the system."
  • "Post-mortems are drift analysis, not failure analysis."
A Couple of Extra Ideas — from the older 2025 edition
  • Managed vs. build-your-own observability: below ~20 pipelines, a custom three-SLO query plus a simple alert webhook is usually cheaper than any managed platform. Past ~100 pipelines, buying often becomes the more sensible option.
  • Spot/interruptible compute as accidental chaos testing: workloads that run on cheaper, interruptible compute often end up more resilient, because random interruptions force real answers to "can this safely resume?" before a real incident does. Checkpointing, atomic writes, and clean resumability are what make a workload safe for cheaper compute.

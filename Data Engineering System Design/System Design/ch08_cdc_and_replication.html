CDC & Replication

CDC isn't really a piece of software — it's a promise. Every insert, update, and especially every delete in the source database shows up, in order, on the other end. Most cheap ways of faking this promise break it silently.

Printed as "Chapter Seven" in the book's own running heads — see the numbering note in Chapter 3. This guide cites it as Chapter 8.
The Simple Version, First

Imagine your company has a "real" database where customers place orders and update profiles. Somewhere else, an analytics warehouse is supposed to show a faithful copy of that same information.

CDC just means: instead of periodically re-copying the whole database, tell me every single thing that changed, the moment it changes. Most cheap ways of faking this promise (like a nightly copy job) quietly break it in ways nobody notices until the numbers are visibly wrong.

Event sourcing and CDC are the same problem solved by different teams. One is my own writes as events, the other is someone else's.
Polling never captures DELETEs. Every replication built on a nightly SELECT diverges from its source over time. The only question is when someone notices.

Why Two Teams Building "The Same Thing" Get Very Different Results

Two teams both need to keep an analytics warehouse in sync with production Postgres. Both are staffed with competent engineers. Both ship in year one.

Team A runs a nightly copy job — simple, well understood. Team B continuously reads the database's own internal change log and streams every change within a minute.

Year one, both look fine. Year two, Team A gets a call from finance: account balances don't match. Every time a customer's data was deleted, that row just vanished from the source — but a nightly "copy everything currently there" job never notices something is now missing. The warehouse quietly kept 340,000 phantom customers who'd left over a year earlier.

Team B had sub-minute freshness, handled deletions correctly, and sailed through two schema changes without incident. The difference wasn't skill — it was what each approach was actually capable of promising.

Idea 1: A Nightly Copy Job Can Never Notice Something Was Deleted

Imagine keeping a filing cabinet that mirrors someone else's. Once a night, you copy everything currently in their cabinet into yours. Now imagine they remove a folder entirely — shredded, returned, legally destroyed. Next time you copy "everything currently there," you simply won't see that folder. But nothing ever told you to remove it from your own cabinet either — so it just stays forever.

❌ Anti-Pattern

Using a nightly full-table copy as your replication strategy for anything that can be updated or deleted. It's quietly wrong for every deletion, and becomes painfully slow once the source table passes about 10 TB.

Idea 2: Read the Database's Own Diary Instead of Asking It Questions

Every real database keeps an internal, ultra-reliable record of everything that's ever happened to it — inserts, updates, deletes, in exact order. Postgres calls this the WAL (write-ahead log); MySQL calls its version the binlog.

💡 The one idea to remember

Instead of periodically asking the database "what changed?", just quietly read its own diary. That diary is authoritative — the exact same record the database trusts for its own crash recovery — so deletions get captured just as reliably as everything else.

Debezium is the standard open-source tool that does this — it tails the WAL and turns every change into a structured event with a consistent shape: the row's value before, its value after, and what kind of change it was.

Idea 4: "Exactly-Once" Only Holds True If the Destination Cooperates

Debezium reliably delivers events at least once — a message might arrive twice, but never silently vanish. Whether a duplicate causes a duplicate row in your warehouse is entirely up to how the destination is built.

sql-- src/code-examples/ch07/apply_cdc_merge.sql
-- Apply a small batch of CDC events safely. Take the newest event
-- per primary key and MERGE it in — duplicates become harmless no-ops.
MERGE INTO warehouse.users AS t
USING (
    SELECT
        COALESCE(after.user_id, before.user_id) AS user_id,
        after.email, after.plan_tier, op, ts_ms,
        ROW_NUMBER() OVER (
            PARTITION BY COALESCE(after.user_id, before.user_id)
            ORDER BY ts_ms DESC
        ) AS rn
    FROM cdc_events_users
    WHERE event_date = CURRENT_DATE
) AS s
ON t.user_id = s.user_id AND s.rn = 1
WHEN MATCHED AND s.op = 'd' THEN DELETE
WHEN MATCHED AND s.op IN ('c', 'u') THEN UPDATE SET
    email = s.email, plan_tier = s.plan_tier,
    updated_at = FROM_UNIXTIME(s.ts_ms / 1000)
WHEN NOT MATCHED AND s.op IN ('c', 'u') THEN INSERT (
    user_id, email, plan_tier, updated_at
) VALUES (
    s.user_id, s.email, s.plan_tier, FROM_UNIXTIME(s.ts_ms / 1000)
);
❌ Anti-Pattern

Trusting Debezium's "exactly-once" claim without checking whether your destination is idempotent. It delivers at-least-once to Kafka — turning that into true end-to-end exactly-once is the destination's job.

✅ Say this out loud

"Debezium's exactly-once claim is only honest when the sink is idempotent. Without that, it's at-least-once with good intentions."

Idea 5: The First-Time Copy Costs More Than People Expect

Before streaming changes, CDC needs a starting point — a full copy of the table as it exists right now, called a snapshot. This is often the most underestimated cost in the whole setup.

Snapshot time scales with data size: roughly 500 GB ≈ 2–6 hours; 10 TB can take multiple days. Teams that don't budget for this end up with an accidental "free" SLA on day one, then spend their first week explaining why the warehouse looks empty.

✅ Pattern

Make the destination idempotent before you trust any "exactly-once" claim. The MERGE pattern (latest event per primary key wins) is the actual answer — not whatever a vendor's marketing page promises.

A Real Interview, Walked Through Simply

The standard CDC prompt: a live production database, a warehouse target, a freshness requirement. Watch the candidate work through log access, schema evolution, destination safety, and the snapshot cost — and correct their own plan mid-answer.

Interviewer

Replicate a live Postgres database to a columnar warehouse. Two hundred tables, 500 GB total, sub-minute freshness, schema changes expected over the next six months.

Candidate

Before I sketch anything — four questions. Can we turn on the database's internal replication log for reading? Of the 200 tables, how many genuinely need sub-minute freshness? Who owns schema changes on the source side? And does the warehouse support an insert-or-update operation?

Interviewer

Yes, approved. About 30 tables are customer-facing and need sub-minute; the other 170 can be slower. No formal schema agreement — the app team just merges changes as part of normal feature work. Warehouse is Iceberg on S3 with Trino, and yes, it supports that operation.

Candidate

Good — so that's really two pipelines, not one. And the schema-change safety net is something we have to build, not just configure.

Here's the shape: a log-reading tool on Postgres, 30 hot tables flowing into their own feeds keyed by primary key, the other 170 through a slower once-a-day path. Schema registry with "safe changes only" turned on. Warehouse applies changes every 30 seconds.

(pauses) Actually, let me walk that back. I said the other 170 could go through a slower "just copy everything nightly" path — but "slow-moving" and "never has anything deleted" aren't the same thing. Any of those 170 tables that can have a row removed would silently drift wrong under a plain nightly copy, exactly like the phantom-customer problem. I'd run all 200 tables through the same log-reading approach, and only change how often each group gets written to the destination — every 30 seconds for the hot 30, once nightly for the rest. Same safe approach, different delivery speed, and deletions get handled correctly everywhere.

Interviewer

What if this pipeline goes down for 24 hours?

Candidate

Two outcomes. If the database's retained log history covers that gap, the pipeline resumes and catches up — duplicates during catch-up are harmless since the destination is idempotent. If retention didn't cover it, we're forced into a full rebuild, which for 500 GB costs around five hours. I'd size log retention for at least seven days specifically so a 24-hour outage never forces that rebuild.

Common Mistakes People Make

  1. Assuming a periodic "copy everything" query captures deletions. It doesn't. Every replication built on a periodic query quietly drifts from its source.
  2. Trusting "exactly-once" without checking the destination. The pipeline gives at-least-once; making the final result exactly-once is the destination's job.
  3. Skipping the schema registry. "We'll manage changes as they come" doesn't work — a registry is the only enforcement that catches problems before deployment.
  4. Forgetting the cost of the first full copy. 500 GB ≈ 5 hours; 10 TB can be multiple days. Missing this from your timeline turns week one into a fire drill.
  5. Mixing multiple tables into a single shared feed. Breaks ordering guarantees and makes correct processing impossible.
Cheat Sheet
Built to be photographed before your interview.

Three Ways to Do CDC

  • Log-based (default) — reads the database's own log directly
  • Query-based — silently misses deletions
  • Trigger-based — portable, but slows every write

The Event Shape

Operation type (create/update/delete), value before, value after, timestamp for ordering. One feed per source table.

Schema Compatibility Modes

  • BACKWARD — new reads old, the common default
  • FORWARD — old reads new, less common
  • FULL — both, strictest
  • NONE — no validation

Making the Destination Safe

  1. Idempotent MERGE on primary key — duplicates become no-ops
  2. Two-phase commit — cleaner, harder to implement

Initial Snapshot Cost

500 GB ≈ 3–5 hours. 10 TB ≈ 2–4 days. Budget this into the timeline explicitly.

Three Lines Worth Memorizing

  • "Log-based CDC beats query-based at every scale."
  • "Polling never captures deletions."
  • "Exactly-once lives in the sink, not the broker."
A Couple of Extra Ideas — from the older 2025 edition
  • CDC's rough edges in practice: schema evolution needs ongoing tracking, repartitioning a source table can cause historical inconsistencies, and some SaaS-hosted databases don't expose their internal logs publicly at all.
  • CDC as the backbone of everything downstream: lakehouse tables need insert-or-update support, feature stores rely on change-based updates, and backfills can reuse the same CDC logs to rebuild tables from scratch.
  • Event sourcing, the close cousin: the same idea — a replayable log of everything that happened — shows up as "event sourcing" when a team designs their own system to emit events for their own writes. CDC is for someone else's database; event sourcing is for your own.

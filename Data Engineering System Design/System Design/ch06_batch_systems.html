Batch Systems

A batch job is just a big task that runs overnight and has to finish before someone needs it. This chapter is really about one idea: things break sometimes, and a good batch system is built assuming that — not hoping around it.

Printed as "Chapter Five" in the book's own running heads — see the numbering note in Chapter 3. This guide cites it as Chapter 6.
The Simple Version, First

Think of a batch job like a bakery prepping bread overnight so it's ready by 6 AM. It has to finish on a schedule, before someone needs the result.

The whole chapter comes down to this: a batch pipeline that only works when nothing breaks isn't a real system — it's a demo. Everything below builds on that one idea.

What you'll be able to say by the end (the book's own scripted lines)
In Spark, shuffle is the cost model. Every optimization is "shuffle less" or "shuffle smarter."
The SLA isn't the deadline. It's the deadline minus the recovery window minus the operator paging budget.
Broadcast joins beat shuffle joins most of the time. Knowing when they don't, and which shuffle strategy fits, is the other half of the answer.
A nightly DAG needs 99% reliability with a 30-minute backfill window, not 100% reliability.
Every operator has to be partition-idempotent. A job that can't be safely rerun on a single failed partition isn't a batch system; it's a one-shot script with a cron.

Why Two Teams With the Same Job Have Very Different Nights

Imagine two teams. Both run a huge nightly job — about 500 terabytes of data in, 50 terabytes of useful summary data out. Both jobs usually finish on time. Both teams are equally skilled.

But one team gets woken up at 2 AM twice a month. The other gets woken up once every three months.

Same code, same size job, same cluster. So what's different?

The team that sleeps better planned for the bad nights. They assumed the job would occasionally break partway through, and built the system so fixing it is quick. The other team assumed things would keep working — and when they didn't, there was no fast way to recover.

Three things make the difference:

  1. Understand what actually costs time and money — almost always, it's moving data between machines.
  2. Pick the right way to combine two datasets — the wrong choice can turn a 2-hour job into a 20-hour one.
  3. Design so a failure only costs a little time to fix, not a full redo.

Idea 1: Most of the Cost Is Moving Data Around, Not Computing

Picture organizing a company potluck: 10,000 people, 10,000 dishes, and you need to match each person to their dish. If names and dishes are already sitting next to each other, it's fast. But if names are scattered across 50 rooms in the building, someone has to physically carry information between rooms first. That carrying between rooms is the expensive part — not the matching itself.

In Spark, this "carrying information between rooms" is called a shuffle. It happens whenever the system needs to group matching things together — like combining two tables on a shared ID, or adding up totals per customer.

💡 The one idea to remember

Shuffling data between machines is slow and expensive. When someone asks "how do I make this job faster," think "how much data is moving, and can I reduce that?" — not "should I add more computers?"

What actually happens during a shuffle

  • Writing side — each machine writes its data to disk, sorted into buckets, one per machine that needs it next. Picture 200 machines each writing 200 separate piles of paper.
  • Moving side — every receiving machine collects its bucket from every writer. With 200 writers and 200 receivers, that's up to 40,000 tiny file transfers — real network traffic, gigabytes to terabytes per job.
  • Combining side — each machine merges what it collected and does the real work. If it gets handed more than fits in memory, it spills to disk — much slower, and usually the moment a job starts to crawl.

One setting that trips people up: spark.sql.shuffle.partitions controls how many buckets data splits into. It defaults to 200 — chosen years ago for much smaller data. Shuffling 300 TB into only 200 buckets means each machine holds 1.5 TB — way too much. Aim for 128 MB–1 GB per bucket and let Spark's automatic tuning (AQE) adjust at runtime.

When one bucket gets way more than its fair share

Picture a call center: most agents get a normal number of calls, but one agent handling a huge VIP account gets ten times as many. Everyone else clocks out on time; that one agent is still on the phone hours later, and nobody can go home until they're done.

That's what happens when one group (one huge customer) has way more data than everyone else. That bucket becomes a bottleneck the whole job has to wait for. This is called skew.

🚩 FAANG Signal

When an interviewer asks "how would you optimize this Spark job?" they want to hear you talk about shuffling first — how much data is moving, and whether it's spread evenly. If your first instinct is "give it more memory" or "add more machines," that tells them you're fixing the wrong problem.

# Give every row on the big side a random number from 0-15 ("salt").
# Copy the small side 16 times, once per possible salt value.
# Now the one giant customer's rows are split across 16 machines
# instead of piling onto just one.
N = 16
fact_salted = fact.withColumn("salt", F.floor(F.rand() * N).cast("int"))
dim_fanned = dim.withColumn(
    "salt", F.explode(F.array([F.lit(i) for i in range(N)]))
)
joined = fact_salted.join(dim_fanned, on=["merchant_id", "salt"])
❌ Anti-Pattern

Copying a table you assumed was small, without checking its real size in production. It works fine in testing and crashes the very first time it runs on real data.

✅ Say this out loud

"I'd broadcast the lookup table, and I've checked that it's under 2 GB with real production data — not just in testing."

Idea 3: A Good Batch Job Assumes Things Will Break

If a job runs perfectly 99 times out of 100, that still means it fails about 3 to 4 nights a year. That's just math. A job only built to work on the 99 good nights isn't finished. Play with the calculator below — it's the exact math from the chapter's worked example.

❌ Anti-Pattern

Scheduling a job to finish right at the deadline, with zero buffer. "It runs from midnight to 4 AM and the report is ready at 4:05." The first time anything goes wrong, there's no room left to fix it before people notice.

Making a failure cheap to fix

  • Make reruns safe to repeat. Write each day's results into its own clearly separate folder that can be safely replaced — so rerunning doesn't create duplicates.
  • Save your work partway through. Break long jobs into stages that save progress, so a failure at stage 3 only means redoing stage 3.
  • Only redo the broken piece. If only Tuesday failed, only rerun Tuesday — not the whole month.
⚠️ War Story

A company ran a huge nightly job (500 terabytes) with a 4-hour deadline. It had worked fine for a year and a half. Then one night, a small upstream change (someone added a field that turned out to be much larger than expected) caused the job to run out of memory four hours in. Because the job wasn't broken into separate, saveable stages, the only way to recover was to rerun the entire thing from scratch — five more hours, blowing right past the deadline. The actual bug was small and boring. The real problem was that the job had no cheap way to recover. The fix was to break the job into stages that save progress, so a failure three-quarters of the way through only costs 45 minutes instead of starting over.

✅ Pattern

A good rule of thumb: if a single day's data fails, fixing it should take less than 25% of the job's normal total runtime. If fixing one bad day takes almost as long as the whole job, you won't be able to recover before the next deadline hits.

A Real Interview, Walked Through Simply

Notice how the candidate asks questions before designing anything, does simple math out loud, and isn't afraid to correct themselves mid-answer.

Interviewer

Design the nightly batch pipeline for a 500 TB event dataset. Daily ingestion. 4-hour deadline. Needs to recover within 1 hour if something breaks.

Candidate

Before I sketch anything — a few questions. What's the final output, summary tables or raw daily data? What kind of matching am I doing — big data against small reference tables, or two big datasets against each other? Is the data split up by day? And is there a known case where one customer makes up way more data than everyone else?

Interviewer

Summary tables plus an ML rollup. Big data against two small reference tables. Daily folders. And yes — a handful of merchants make up 40% of all the data.

Candidate

Good — since both reference tables are small, I can copy them to every machine and skip the expensive shuffle for that part entirely.

Quick math: 500 TB in, maybe 50 TB out after summarizing. The expensive part is probably around 300 TB once we drop unneeded columns.

Time budget: 4 hours total, minus 1 hour recovery, minus 30 minutes to notice and restart — so really 2.5 hours for a clean run. Moving 300 TB in 2.5 hours needs about 33 GB every second. At roughly 750 MB/sec per machine, that's about 45 machines — I'd plan for 60 to 80 with margin.

(pauses) Actually, let me double check that. Splitting 300 TB into the default 200 buckets means each machine holds 1.5 TB — way too much. I'd target 256 MB per bucket instead, about 1.2 million smaller buckets, and let automatic tuning combine them. That favors machines with more cores — so 40 machines at 16 cores and 64 GB each, instead of 60 at 8 cores. Same total power, better shape for this shuffle.

Interviewer

What about that skewed merchant data?

Candidate

Matching against users is fine, pretty even. But summarizing by merchant, that one merchant at 40% would pile onto one machine. I'd split it using the salting trick — summarize by (merchant, random-split-number) first, spreading the work out, then a second smaller step combines those pieces back into one number per merchant. At 40% I'd do this by hand rather than trust the automatic fix.

Interviewer

How do you recover if something fails? What would you watch?

Candidate

Each day writes to its own folder, so a rerun just replaces that day — no duplicates. I'd split the job into two save-points, after matching and after summarizing, so a late failure doesn't redo the matching step. That brings recovery from 2.5 hours down to about 45 minutes. For monitoring, I'd watch three slow-changing things — stage runtime, shuffle volume, and output row count — so a sudden jump is easy to catch before it becomes an outage.

Common Mistakes People Make

  1. Talking about "how many machines" before "how much data is shuffling." The machine count should come from the shuffle math, not before it.
  2. Assuming a "small" table is actually small, without checking. Small in testing, 4 GB in production, and the job crashes.
  3. Trusting the automatic skew fix for extreme cases. Fine for mild imbalance. A customer at 40% usually needs the manual fix.
  4. Scheduling the job to finish exactly at the deadline. No buffer means the first real failure becomes a missed deadline in front of everyone.
  5. Building one giant job with no save-points. Any failure means starting completely over.
Cheat Sheet
Built to be photographed before your interview.

The One-Sentence Version

Moving data between machines is the expensive part. Everything else is smaller by comparison.

Your Real Time Budget

real budget = deadline − notice/restart − rerun time

Noticing and restarting takes ~30 min on average. A good rerun takes ~25% of the normal full runtime.

Three Ways to Combine Data

  • Broadcast — copy the small table, check its real size first
  • Shuffle-and-sort — the reliable default, both sides move
  • Salted — split up one giant key, use above ~10% share

Two Settings Worth Knowing

spark.sql.shuffle.partitions = 200 (too small at scale — aim for 128 MB–1 GB per bucket instead)

spark.sql.autoBroadcastJoinThreshold = 10 MB (production often raises to 100–200 MB+)

Three Habits That Make Failures Cheap

  • Each day's data lives in its own replaceable folder
  • Long jobs save progress at each stage
  • A single bad day only needs that day rerun

Three Lines Worth Memorizing

  • "Moving data between machines is the cost. Everything else is noise."
  • "The deadline isn't the time budget. It's the deadline minus recovery."
  • "If a job can't safely rerun one bad day, it isn't a batch system yet."
A Couple of Extra Ideas — from the older 2025 edition
  • Spark vs. Flink for a fast (5-minute) deadline: a trade-off between speed, operational simplicity, and reusing the same logic later for reprocessing old data — valuable when a business team asks for a redo with different rules.
  • Cutting costs on an always-running job by ~30%: mixing cheaper, less-guaranteed compute with regular compute; picking memory-heavy machines for memory-heavy work; a more disk-friendly way to store in-progress state; keeping only the most recent day's data "hot."
  • A simpler way to avoid double-counting in batch jobs: a basic "remove duplicate IDs" step early on, as long as every event has one guaranteed unique ID.

Cost at Scale

Cutting a data platform's bill by 30–40% is almost never about getting a better vendor deal. It's about finding which queries scan more than they need to, and moving the right data to a cheaper shelf.

Printed as "Chapter Thirteen" in the book's own running heads — see the numbering note in Chapter 3. This guide cites it as Chapter 14.
The Simple Version, First

Two roommates both want to cut the household bill. One negotiates a slightly better electricity rate. The other actually walks through the house and finds the water heater's been running full blast 24/7 for no reason.

Negotiating helps a little. Finding the actual waste helps a lot. That's the whole chapter — cost optimization is a design discipline, not a billing one.

What you'll be able to say by the end (the book's own scripted lines)
Storage cost grows linearly; compute cost grows with the square of optimization neglect. A query that scans a partition isn't the same as a query that scans the table.
Cold tiering pays for itself the first time someone needs 90-day retention on a dataset that's hot-queried for two days after ingestion.
Reserved vs. on-demand is a bet about workload predictability. I can state the forecast error I'm willing to accept.
Cost per query is a user-level metric, not an aggregate. Five percent of users cause eighty percent of the bill, and you can't fix that by tuning the warehouse.
The best compute cost is the compute I didn't spend. Every dollar saved on a recurring query is saved every time it runs.

Why Two Teams With the Same Starting Bill Ended Up Nowhere Near Each Other

Two platforms, same tech stack, both paying $1.2M a month. Team A's finance lead negotiated a 15% vendor discount — saved $180k a month, everyone celebrated.

Team B identified their top 20 most expensive queries (1% of volume, 60% of compute cost), pre-computed the worst five, moved 70% of cold data to a cheap archive tier, and switched 60% of compute to discounted reserved capacity. They saved $480k a month and never talked to a vendor.

Same stack, same starting bill. Team B treated cost as a design problem. Team A treated it as a billing problem.

❌ Anti-Pattern

Treating cost optimization as vendor negotiation. A vendor discount is capped at whatever margin they're willing to give up — usually 10–20%. Architectural headroom underneath is often 2x to 5x that, and entirely in your own control. Negotiation is a closing move, not an opening one.

Idea 1: Where the Money Actually Goes

A typical data platform bill, in order of size: Compute (50–70%) — the biggest and most variable lever. Storage (20–30%) — steady, predictable, the easiest lever. Data movement (5–15%) — easy to forget until it's large. Third-party tools (5–15%) — often growing fastest.

Optimization effort should follow the line items — not whichever one is easiest to talk about in a meeting.

Idea 2: Storage Cost Is Simple. Compute Cost Is Not.

Storage is linear — twice the data, roughly twice the cost. Compute is not, in two separate ways: cost scales with bytes scanned (not query complexity), and cost compounds with how often a query runs.

A $1 query run hourly costs ~$730/month. A $50 query run hourly costs ~$36,000/month. The same absolute fix saves 10x more on the expensive, frequent query.

sql-- src/code-examples/ch13/cost_attribution.sql
-- Rank users by 30-day compute cost. Typical shape: top 10 users
-- = 60% of the bill, top 20 = 80%.
WITH user_query_stats AS (
    SELECT
        user_email, COUNT(*) AS query_count,
        SUM(bytes_scanned) AS total_bytes_scanned,
        ROUND(SUM(bytes_scanned) / POWER(2, 40) * 5.00, 2) AS est_cost_usd
    FROM warehouse.query_history
    WHERE start_time >= CURRENT_DATE - INTERVAL '30' DAY
    GROUP BY user_email
)
SELECT user_email, query_count, est_cost_usd,
    RANK() OVER (ORDER BY est_cost_usd DESC) AS cost_rank
FROM user_query_stats
ORDER BY cost_rank LIMIT 20;

⚠️ War Story

A data platform team at a consumer SaaS company was paying $900k a month in warehouse costs. A single day building a cost-attribution report revealed 62% of the bill came from just 14 users — nine running BI dashboards against raw tables, three running ad-hoc queries asking for every column against tables organized for a different filter pattern, two doing legitimate ML prep. The fix: pre-computed summary tables for the dashboards (one week), a per-query cost alert (two days), and an office-hours session with the three ad-hoc users (one afternoon). Nobody talked to a vendor. Six weeks later the bill was $420k — a 53% reduction. The users weren't doing anything malicious; nobody had ever shown them what their own queries cost.

Idea 5: Knowing Who's Actually Spending the Money

A single aggregate bill hides where money goes. Per-user, per-query attribution is the single most useful cost report — it turns "the warehouse is expensive" into "this pipeline costs $80/month and the owner can cut it 60% with a one-week refactor."

Three things a useful attribution system needs: per-query cost (already tracked by most warehouses' system logs), per-user rollups, and monthly budget alerts at 50/80/100% — that trigger a conversation, not a block.

✅ Pattern

Ship per-user cost dashboards before any cost-reduction effort. Attribution is the precondition; optimization follows. The cheapest way to save $100k a month is often showing the top five spenders their own numbers.

A Real Interview, Walked Through Simply

The classic cost prompt: a specific savings target, no capability loss, no vendor migration. Watch the candidate sequence by speed of payoff — and catch their own reservation math mid-answer.

Interviewer

Cut the monthly bill by 40% without reducing capabilities or migrating vendors. Current bill: $1.2M/month.

Candidate

Is the bill growing, flat, or shrinking? What does cost attribution look like today? Is there existing storage tiering?

Interviewer

Growing 8% a quarter. Rough team-level attribution only. Everything on the standard storage tier, no tiering policy.

Candidate

So this is cost cut plus growth control. Four workstreams, sequenced by payoff speed: (1) attribution — ship a per-user dashboard in 2 weeks, the precondition for everything else. (2) storage tiering — auto-tier cold data, ~$120k/month saved, ~10% of bill, low risk. (3) query optimization — materialize and rewrite the top 20 expensive queries, ~$200k/month, ~17% of bill. (4) reservation tuning — commit 70% of the new baseline after optimization lands.

(pauses) Let me actually sanity-check that reservation number instead of just asserting it. 70% reserved at a 30% discount is 21% savings on that portion — on a post-optimization $600k/month bill, that's roughly $126k saved. Matches my estimate. But our 8%-per-quarter growth could push us past the commitment within the year — if we grow 32% over the coming year, the overage eats into the discount.

Actually, I'd revise down to 60% committed instead of 70% — about $100k saved instead of $126k, but it protects us if growth accelerates.

Cumulative: ~36%, close to but short of 40%. I'd add a fifth workstream: (5) dashboard refresh renegotiation — most dashboards refresh every 15 minutes by default; auditing the top 10 and moving the ones that don't need sub-hour freshness to hourly/daily cuts dashboard compute ~80%, roughly $80k/month. Total: ~43%.

Interviewer

What are the risks?

Candidate

Reservation timing — commit after optimization lands, not before, or we lock in excess capacity based on the old, wasteful baseline. Dashboard refresh pushback — needs executive backing, since the loudest users tend to overstate how much freshness they actually need. Attribution gaming — once users see their own costs, some will route through shared service accounts to hide. Need a tagging policy before attribution ships.

Common Mistakes People Make

  1. Starting with vendor negotiation. The discount ceiling is their margin, usually 10–20%. The architecture's ceiling is 2x to 5x that.
  2. Optimizing without attribution first. You cut something visible, not something expensive. Net savings are close to nothing.
  3. Committing to reserved capacity without measuring forecast error. You commit to capacity the workload won't use — the penalty cancels the discount.
  4. Deferring storage tiering. The easiest win, left on the table because nobody scheduled the short job to set it up.
  5. Treating cost as a quarterly project instead of an ongoing habit. Savings regress within 2–3 quarters without a monthly review.

Where the Money Goes

Compute 50–70% · Storage 20–30% · Data movement 5–15% · Third-party tools 5–15%

Storage Tiers (~10x Apart)

Hot ~$23/TB/mo · Warm ~$12 · Cold ~$4 · Archive ~$1

Default: hot 30 days, warm to 180, cold/archive after.

Compute, Two Dimensions

  • Scales with bytes scanned, not query complexity
  • Compounds with frequency — prioritize (cost × runs)

Reservation by Forecast Error

  • Under 10% error → commit 80–90%
  • 10–30% error → commit 50–70%
  • Over 30% error → minimal or none

Optimization Priority Order

  1. Build attribution (~2 weeks)
  2. Materialize top 5–10 recurring queries
  3. Rewrite function-on-partition, SELECT * anti-patterns
  4. Route dashboards to pre-aggregates
  5. Tune reservations, after the above

A Couple of Extra Ideas — from the older 2025 edition
  • Spot/interruptible compute as a deliberate cost lever: fault-tolerant, non-urgent workloads (backfills, ETL rewrites, ML training, compaction) can run 70–100% on cheaper interruptible compute, at 60–90% lower cost. Latency-sensitive or stateful workloads stay on guaranteed capacity. Most platforms blend both.
  • Serverless vs. dedicated clusters is a separate cost trade-off: serverless is cost-effective for bursty, short-lived work with no idle cost, but can get expensive at high sustained volume since it doesn't benefit from a long-running cluster's caching or locality. Real platforms typically run both — serverless for ingestion and ad-hoc queries, clusters for continuous heavy lifting.

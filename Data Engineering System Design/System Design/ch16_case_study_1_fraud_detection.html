System Design for Data Engineers · Chapter 16 · Case Study 1

Real-Time Fraud Detection

A payment is trying to happen right now. The system has a tiny fraction of a second to decide "let this through" or "block this" — and the customer is waiting, because the payment doesn't complete until the answer comes back.

Chapter 16 is printed as "Chapter Fifteen" in the book's own running heads — see the numbering note in Chapter 3. This is the first of four full case studies.
The Simple Version, First

A bouncer at an exclusive club has exactly one second to decide whether to let someone in — no do-overs, and the doorway is genuinely blocked until the decision is made. Now imagine that call has to be made correctly, thousands of times per second, for people arriving from all over the world.

This case study is where everything from earlier chapters comes together at once — skew, streaming design, orchestration discipline, reliability thinking — and just as importantly, the interview performance skills from Chapter 15. The architecture matters, but it's necessary, not sufficient.

The Prompt

"Design a real-time fraud detection system for a payment company. Peak traffic is in the thousands of transactions per second. The decision — fraud or not — has to come back within one hundred milliseconds. The decision is blocking: the transaction doesn't complete until we've scored it. We also keep a seven-day reversal window in case we get something wrong and need to undo it later."

Idea 2: Naming the Weak Point — With a Specific Number

Once the constraints are clear, a strong candidate states, explicitly, which part of the system they expect to break first — and why, using the actual numbers from the conversation.

✅ Say this out loud

"Given the budget is a hundred milliseconds end-to-end — network hop, feature retrieval, scoring, write-back — I'd expect feature-store tail latency to break first. Specifically, the p99.9 on the feature store."

Not "I'm worried about latency" — that's vague, and vague statements don't stick. Saying specifically "Redis p99.9 sustained above five milliseconds" is the gap between reading about a system and having actually operated one.

Idea 3: Solving Skew With a Smarter Key, Not a Bigger Hammer

If the system organizes work by merchant ID, the top 1% of merchants — carrying 40% of volume — pile onto the same small number of partitions. Compare the two approaches below.

Two ways to partition the same transaction stream

❌ Partition by merchant_id

Top 1% of merchants = 40% of volume. One worker gets pinned at 100% CPU while the rest sit idle. Needs salting to fix.

✅ Partition by card_id

Each card belongs to one person who spends across many merchants — activity naturally spreads across the long tail. No salting needed, and per-card velocity (the main fraud signal) stays correctly grouped.

🚩 FAANG Signal

Three things signal seniority here: the math came before the tool choice, the skew observation led to a non-obvious fix (card ID, not the "obvious" merchant-ID salting), and the weak dimension was a specific metric — not a vague word.

Idea 6: Handling Private Data Without Slowing Anything Down

  • Keep raw personal information out of the fast path entirely. Only hashed identifiers flow through the real-time pipeline. Anything derived from personal information is pre-computed upstream by a system that's allowed to see it.
  • Handle deletion requests as a specific, traceable event. A "tombstone" event, keyed by the hashed identifier, tells the stream processor to remove all state for that identifier — not a manual cleanup task someone remembers to run.

The 30-Second Closing Summary

Volunteered, unprompted, right around minute 38 of a 45-minute interview — the move from Chapter 15 in action.

Candidate — What I'd build

A stream-processing pipeline partitioned by card ID for natural skew avoidance, a fast in-memory store for recent-activity features with a 1-millisecond target, a slower columnar store for baseline features refreshed hourly, a model server co-located for latency, and a client-supplied idempotency key so retries are always safe.

What I'd sacrifice

Operational complexity. The fast in-memory store is a single point of failure for the whole latency target — I'd invest in monitoring it closely from day one. Model deployments are currently coupled to the stream processor's own deploys because they're co-located; that's a refactor trigger once the ML team's release pace picks up.

What I'd watch, in priority order

Feature-store worst-case latency under 5ms (primary alert), checkpoint duration relative to the checkpoint interval (secondary), dead-letter queue rate (catches upstream schema drift), and agreement rate between the live model and a shadow "challenger" model over a rolling week (catches model drift).

Two questions for the interviewer

"How does this team currently detect model drift — an existing shared pattern, or usually custom-built per ML team?" And: "What's the real ratio between transactions blocked in real time versus fraud caught later through reversal? That tells me where investment should actually go."

What This Case Study Is Really Teaching

The architecture above is solid but not unique — other strong candidates would sketch something similar. What actually separates a strong answer isn't the diagram. It's the sequence of moves:

  1. Four sharp clarifying questions before drawing anything, each genuinely changing the design
  2. The weak dimension named early, with a specific number attached
  3. A non-obvious fix for skew, showing real pattern recognition
  4. A specific idempotency mechanism, triggered by a specific clarifying answer
  5. Graceful degradation named explicitly for two separate failure modes
  6. Privacy handled as a first-class concern, not bolted on at the end
  7. A volunteered, unprompted 30-second summary landing exactly on time
  8. Two thoughtful closing questions probing how the team actually operates

Common Mistakes People Make

  1. Jumping straight to naming tools. Skips the entire signal the interviewer is listening for first.
  2. Assuming traffic is evenly spread. Real payment traffic is almost always long-tailed — designing as if uniform means rebuilding the partitioning strategy later, under pressure.
  3. Treating "I'll make it idempotent" as enough. Name the specific mechanism, tied to the retry answer.
  4. Saying "I'll handle failures" instead of naming a specific fallback. Vague promises don't land.
  5. Running out of time before the summary. Often what an interviewer remembers most clearly.
Cheat Sheet
Built to be photographed before your interview.

Five Questions, What Each Locks In

  1. Blocking vs. advisory? → the real latency budget
  2. Even or long-tailed? → the partitioning strategy
  3. Same decision on retry? → the idempotency mechanism
  4. How heavy is the model? → where the bottleneck lives
  5. Cost ceiling? → what to optimize purely for

Weak Dimension, Stated Right

Not "latency." Instead: "Feature-store tail latency — Redis p99.9 — is what I'd expect to break first."

The Non-Obvious Skew Fix

Partition by card_id, not merchant_id. One card spreads across many merchants naturally — no salting needed.

Two Graceful-Degradation Rules

  • Slow lookup → flag, fall back, reconcile later
  • Model down → circuit breaker at ~5%, rules-only, reprocess

Privacy, Two Moves

  • Only hashed IDs touch the real-time path
  • Deletion = a tombstone event, not manual cleanup

Three Lines Worth Memorizing

  • "That answer locks in the latency budget — let me confirm it first."
  • "The weak dimension is feature-store tail latency, specifically."
  • "Never stall on a slow dependency — flag, fall back, reconcile."
A Couple of Extra Ideas — from the older 2025 edition
  • A second framing of this same prompt uses 50,000 transactions/second with a 2–3 second budget instead of 100ms, and treats false negatives as strictly worse than false positives — meaning the system should lean toward flagging more when genuinely uncertain.
  • Historical-join scaling tactics: sharding the lookup store by a hash of the ID, caching the highest-frequency identifiers directly in stream workers' memory, and using approximate structures (Bloom filters, HyperLogLog) when an exact count isn't necessary.
  • A concrete disaster-recovery stress test: queue corruption loses 30 minutes of data, the SLA breaches during that window, a regulator asks for an explanation. Strong answers name a specific recovery time objective and recovery point objective, and treat the regulatory conversation as part of the design — immutable incident logs, a clear explanation of the fallback behavior used, and evidence no high-value fraud was missed.

System Design for Data Engineers · Chapter 16 · Case Study 3

Clickstream Analytics at Scale

Product teams ship new features every week. Every large consumer company running clickstream data at scale hits the exact same three problems: producers change data shape without warning, consumers silently break, and the data team gets blamed.

Chapter 16 is printed as "Chapter Fifteen" in the book's own running heads — see the numbering note in Chapter 3. This is the third of four full case studies.
The Simple Version, First

Five departments quietly rely on the same shared filing cabinet — but nobody who fills it tells anyone else when they change how things are labeled. One day someone renames a folder, and three other departments' reports go silently wrong.

Where fraud had a latency ceiling and recommendation had a cost ceiling, this case study has a schema-compatibility ceiling — a genuinely different, counterintuitive shape of constraint for what sounds like "just streaming."

The Prompt

"Design the clickstream analytics platform for a consumer SaaS company. Product engineering teams ship new features weekly, each adding new event types and properties. Five hundred million events per day at peak, growing 20% quarter over quarter. Multiple downstream consumers: product dashboards, marketing attribution, ML training, BI reporting."

Idea 1: The First Question Isn't About Volume — It's About Schema Reality

  1. "Truly new event shapes, or new properties on existing events?" → determines the real scope. Answer: mostly new properties, 2-3 genuinely new events/month.
  2. "Who owns each event type?" → surfaces the organizational gap. Answer: product teams ship, data team consumes, no formal contract today.
  3. "Which downstream engines?" → decides the table format. Answer: an interactive engine, a batch engine, a transform tool, BI, occasional streaming.
  4. "Freshness per consumer?" → shapes speed tiers. Real-time for ML, seconds-to-minutes for dashboards, hourly for attribution, daily for reporting.
  5. "Retention and compliance?" → shapes GDPR handling. Two years hot, seven years cold.

Idea 2: A Counterintuitive Weak Dimension — Committed To

🚩 FAANG Signal

"Schema compatibility is the weak dimension, not throughput or cost" is a counterintuitive claim for a streaming prompt, and it's correct here. The interviewer hears the candidate diagnosed the right dimension for this domain, and stated it with the producer team named as the actual source of risk. A weaker candidate says "schema evolution matters" without committing to it as the weak dimension.

Idea 3: The Architecture, in Plain Terms

  • Producers write events to a queue — but two schema checks fire before anything lands.
  • Check one, at PR time: an automated build check against the schema registry, failing breaking changes with a specific error.
  • Check two, at runtime: the queue's producer library validates against the registered schema — a belt-and-suspenders layer for anything that slips past check one.
  • A stream processor validates, enriches, and writes to the lakehouse's raw zone; malformed events go to a dead-letter queue with a reason code.
  • Two logical zones: raw (append-only, everything that passed validation) and curated (modeled fact/dimension tables built nightly).
  • Multiple engines read from both zones in parallel — the reason table format choice matters so much here.

Idea 4: Why This Table Format, Specifically

✅ Say this out loud

"Writer neutrality. Four different engines write to, or want to write to, the same tables. A Spark-first format makes other engines second-class here. An update-optimized format costs more than it buys us since we're append-heavy. Writer-neutral is the default for exactly this multi-engine situation."

Idea 7: Maintenance and Cost — Applying Earlier Chapters Directly

  • Lakehouse maintenance scheduled from day one — daily compaction, 14-day snapshot expiration, weekly manifest rewrites, monthly orphan cleanup.
  • Cost attribution focuses on the ad-hoc interactive-query engine, not storage — most clickstream spend concentrates there.
  • Materialized views at the curated zone are the single biggest cost lever for BI-driven workloads — 10x to 100x cost reduction on dashboard queries.
  • The function-on-partition-column trap shows up constantly here — analysts reformatting a timestamp instead of comparing the raw partition column.

The 30-Second Closing Summary

✅ What I'd build

A three-zone lakehouse fed by a message queue, with a stream processor validating and enriching. The schema registry with backward compatibility is what the whole design depends on, enforced in two places. Multi-engine consumption drives the table format. GDPR erasure uses the four-part tombstone pattern. Maintenance is scheduled from day one.

✅ What I'd sacrifice

Schema-evolution rigor upfront — the cost of integrating checks into every producer pipeline, and the political cost of rejecting breaking-change PRs. The alternative — quarterly contract-violation postmortems — is more expensive over any horizon longer than six months.

✅ What I'd watch

DLQ rate per event type, file-count-per-partition trend, schema registry manual-registration events, and per-user query-engine cost weekly.

🚩 FAANG Signal

Asking "how does your company handle the friction between the data team and product engineering around schema changes?" bookends the interview — the same organizational insight from Question 2 at the very start, now closing the conversation.

Common Mistakes People Make

  1. Defaulting to throughput as the weak dimension because the prompt sounds like streaming. Diagnose per domain.
  2. Picking a table format by popularity instead of by who's writing.
  3. Treating "we have a schema registry" as sufficient. The enforcement mechanism is what actually prevents incidents.
  4. Forgetting the dead-letter queue in GDPR erasure. A real compliance gap.
  5. Treating schema evolution as purely technical. It's an organizational contract too.

A Couple of Extra Ideas — from the older 2025 edition
  • A 10x traffic spike ("Black Friday") without breaching SLA or blowing up cost: scale ingestion partitions ahead of the spike, compress payloads, decouple raw ingestion from enrichment, and autoscale on backlog depth rather than a fixed schedule.
  • Identity resolution across surfaces: device and session IDs exist everywhere, but a stable user ID may only exist for signed-in users on some surfaces — design for what each consumer can and can't rely on.
  • A "customer 360" serving layer often uses the same raw/curated pattern, with GDPR as a two-step deletion (soft-delete, then physical after confirming propagation) plus a daily reconciliation job.

IoT Telemetry at 1M Sensors/Second

A naive reading of "seven years of retention at one million readings per second" is architecturally impossible at any reasonable cost. The fix isn't a trick bolted on later — it's a core design decision made on day one.

Chapter 16 is printed as "Chapter Fifteen" in the book's own running heads — see the numbering note in Chapter 3. This is the fourth of four full case studies.
The Simple Version, First

Imagine being asked to keep a photograph of every single second of your life for the next seven years. Obviously absurd. What you'd actually want: full detail for anything unusual recently, then progressively fewer representative snapshots as time passes.

Where fraud had a latency ceiling and clickstream had a schema ceiling, this case study has a retention ceiling that makes naive storage infeasible. The lesson: downsampling is a first-class architectural primitive, not a cleanup task.

The Prompt

"Design the data platform for an industrial IoT deployment. One million sensors across thousands of sites, each emitting a reading per second at peak. Real-time anomaly detection within seconds. Historical trend analysis for predictive maintenance. Seven-year retention for regulatory compliance."

Idea 1: Doing the Impossible Math First — Out Loud

"The naive reading — seven years at one million readings per second — is architecturally infeasible, which I'll get to. Before I draw, a few questions specific to IoT."

  1. "Always online, or offline buffering?" → Answer: intermittent connectivity, sensors buffer 48 hours then dump. Late arrivals are normal, not an edge case.
  2. "Does compliance require raw, or do aggregates satisfy it?" → Answer: aggregates are fine. This one answer changes required storage by three orders of magnitude.
  3. "Rule-based, statistical, or learned anomaly model?" → Answer: threshold-based now, learned model on the roadmap.
  4. "Query pattern: aggregation, drill-down, or cross-device correlation?" → Answer: mostly drill-down plus trends.

Idea 4: The Architecture, in Plain Terms

  • The gateway is the edge ingestion point (MQTT or HTTPS). Auth and encryption live here; internal systems only see trusted events.
  • The queue is the durable event log, partitioned by device ID, 3-day retention — the lakehouse is the true system of record.
  • The stream processor runs two jobs simultaneously: anomaly detection against thresholds, and continuous 1-minute downsampling writing straight to the lakehouse.
  • The lakehouse is tiered — each retention window its own table with its own lifecycle policy.
  • A nightly batch job runs the cascading rollup — 1-minute to 1-hour at the 90-day boundary, 1-hour to 1-day at the 1-year boundary.
  • A small database holds device fleet metadata — firmware, calibration, site assignment — queried during anomaly detection.

Idea 5: Handling Late Arrivals — a Normal Pattern

✅ Say this out loud

"1-hour allowed lateness in the watermark strategy is much larger than the 1-second we'd use in fraud detection. IoT connectivity patterns make late arrivals normal, not exceptional."

1-minute aggregates can be revised up to 48 hours after their initial write. Past that, they're immutable — the compliance invariant.

Idea 6: What Breaks First at Production Scale

  1. Checkpoint duration creeping up as the device fleet grows — alert above 20% of the checkpoint interval.
  2. A late-arrival flood after a regional outage — up to ~1.7 billion events queued at once. The backfill job needs its own autoscaling pool sized for ~10x steady-state.
  3. Device fleet metadata drift — firmware and calibration changes causing false positives if the metadata database falls out of sync.

Idea 7: The Real Cost Breakdown

Total IoT infrastructure cost: roughly $65k/month, or ~$780k/year. The downsampling decision by itself is worth more than the entire rest of the infrastructure cost combined.

🚩 FAANG Signal

"The downsampling architecture is worth $10.5 million, and it's the first number I'd put on a design-review slide." A mid-level candidate says "we'll downsample for cost." A staff-level candidate produces the specific dollar figure — that's what the interviewer remembers.

Idea 8: Proving Compliance Without Keeping Raw Data Forever

Chain of custody: every table records lineage back to its source — the message-queue offset range plus the versioned, code-signed aggregation logic that produced it. Regulators accept this without requiring raw data retention, as long as the transformation is reproducible from metadata.

Historical aggregates are never modified after finalization — the compliance invariant. A later-discovered error creates a new table with corrected logic; the old one is preserved, never silently overwritten.

The 30-Second Closing Summary

✅ What I'd build

The message queue as the durable event log, partitioned by device ID. A stream processor running anomaly detection and continuous downsampling simultaneously on the same keyed stream. A tiered lakehouse with cascading nightly rollups handling tier transitions.

✅ What I'd sacrifice

The two stream-processor jobs are coupled in deployment because they run on the same stream. 1-minute aggregates can be revised up to 48 hours after write.

✅ What I'd watch

Checkpoint duration relative to interval, late-arrival backlog size, and device-metadata drift via dead-letter rate cross-referenced against expected firmware.

Common Mistakes People Make

  1. Assuming worst-case retention without confirming it. Confirming whether aggregates satisfy compliance changes the design entirely.
  2. Treating downsampling as an optimization for later. The design is infeasible without it at this scale.
  3. Using the same watermark tolerance as a low-latency use case. Late arrivals are normal in IoT, not an edge case.
  4. Forgetting device fleet metadata. A genuine, ongoing source of false positives if not kept in sync.
  5. Not quantifying the big decision in dollars. A specific number beats a vague statement every time.

A Couple of Extra Ideas — from the older 2025 edition
  • Time-series database storage-tiering patterns apply directly here, even without a purpose-built product — naming the pattern signals a proven approach rather than inventing one under pressure.
  • Always ask whether a retention number applies to raw or some representation. The same requirement is often satisfiable at wildly different costs — confirming this is often the highest-leverage question in the whole conversation.
  • Real IoT-adjacent platforms report the retention/compliance conversation, not the ingestion pipeline, is where the actual budget conversations happen.

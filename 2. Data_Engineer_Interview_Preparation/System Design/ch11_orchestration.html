Orchestration

Orchestration is a contract, not a calendar. Drawing boxes and arrows and picking run times is the easy 20%. The real 80% is what happens when something goes wrong.

The Simple Version, First

A busy restaurant kitchen doesn't just hand out a printed schedule and walk away. The real job is what happens when the delivery truck is late or two orders hit the grill at once. A schedule that only works when nothing goes wrong isn't a kitchen — it's a poster on the wall.

Orchestration is a contract, not a calendar. The real work is designing for what happens when something upstream is late, a job succeeds but produces wrong data, or two copies of the same job accidentally try to write the same thing at once.

Why One Team Sleeps Through the Night and Another Doesn't

Two teams each run about 200 scheduled pipelines. Both use the same tool. Both promise the business "99.9% on-time."

Team A gets paged three times a week, incidents dragging two to eight hours. Almost always: something upstream was late, a downstream job timed out, and by the time someone notices, hours have passed.

Team B gets paged twice a month, resolved in under 30 minutes — because the on-call reruns just the broken piece instead of untangling the whole thing.

Same tools, same complexity. Team B treats orchestration as a contract. Team A treats it as scheduling.

Idea 1: A Task That Can't Be Safely Repeated Isn't Ready for Production

Failures are the normal state of any system running continuously — a network hiccup, a worker restart. The question isn't "will this task ever get retried?" It's "what happens when it is?"

A task that appends a row every time it runs will silently duplicate data the first time a retry stacks on top of a partial write. An idempotent task produces the exact same result whether it runs once or ten times — the single most important property in this chapter.

ApproachHow it worksUse it when
Replace, don't appendOverwrites one specific data slice (e.g. "all of June 15th") instead of adding to itData organized by date
Insert-or-update with dedupRemoves duplicates first, then applies an operation keyed on a stable unique IDData with a natural unique key, not date-organized
Write temp, then atomic swapWrites to staging first, swaps into place only when fully doneFile-based output where partial writes must never be visible
❌ Anti-Pattern

"We'll just retry on failure" without making the task idempotent first. Retrying a non-idempotent task is a recipe for silently corrupting data under load.

Idea 2: Retries Are Supposed to Happen — Design for Them on Purpose

Transient failures are the normal state of the world, not an emergency. The sensible default: retry up to 3 times, waiting a bit longer each time (5, then 10, then 20 minutes). This handles roughly 80% of transient pain with zero human involvement — but only works safely once the task is idempotent.

Idea 3: Two Copies of the Same Job at Once Is How Data Gets Silently Wrong

Most tools cap how many copies of the same pipeline can run simultaneously — often defaulting to a fairly high number. That's fine for jobs where parallel copies don't matter. It's very much not fine for a job where each run writes to a shared data slice.

⚠️ War Story

A data platform team ran 200 pipelines. One core pipeline computed daily revenue, with its concurrency cap left at the tool's default of 16. One morning, an upstream data format change caused a dependency check to time out on first run. The system retried — but the scheduler also launched the next day's run, and the one after that, stacking up missed schedules. Within three hours, eight overlapping copies were fighting over the same day's data slice. Each used the safe "replace, don't append" pattern — but each was working from a different snapshot of upstream data, since it kept changing between runs. The final result was whichever copy finished last. The next morning's dashboards showed revenue numbers that made no sense. The investigation took eight hours. The fix was one line: capping concurrent runs at 1. The real lesson: idempotency plus a concurrency cap is the actual safety contract. Either one without the other doesn't protect you.

✅ Pattern

For any pipeline where each run writes to a shared data slice, cap it to exactly one run at a time. Say this out loud unprompted — the too-permissive default is the single most common real-world misconfiguration at medium scale.

Idea 4: Waiting on Something Upstream Needs Its Own Careful Design

A "sensor" waits for something upstream to be ready. A naive sensor holds a worker slot for its entire wait, doing nothing but checking. 100 pipelines all polling this way ties up 100 worker slots doing zero real work — invisible at small scale, a real bottleneck at medium scale.

The fix: release the worker slot between checks instead of holding it the whole time. Newer tools let a finished pipeline directly announce its output is ready, so downstream pipelines don't poll at all.

🚩 FAANG Signal

When you mention a sensor waiting on an upstream dependency, immediately flag the polling-vs-release distinction. Naming it unprompted signals you've run this at a scale where it actually mattered.

Idea 5: When Something Breaks, How Much Do You Have to Redo?

Two very different-sized responses: rebuild everything from scratch (simple, slow, touches data that was already fine), or fix just the broken piece (dramatically faster, but only safe if the task is idempotent). Teams without idempotent tasks default to full rebuilds every time — the difference between a 30-minute fix and an 8-hour one.

✅ Pattern

Every pipeline you ship should come with a rerun plan — the exact command, tested somewhere safe, pasted directly into the on-call runbook.

A Real Interview, Walked Through Simply

The classic orchestration prompt: a specific pipeline count, a specific reliability promise, a specific "how bad can it get" limit. Watch the candidate tier by criticality, budget the recovery math, and reason through what breaks first at scale.

Interviewer

Design the orchestration for a 200-pipeline platform with a 99.9% reliability promise and a 4-hour maximum "how bad can it get" limit.

Candidate

Three questions first. Does 99.9% apply per-pipeline or to the platform as a whole? How critical are these 200 pipelines relative to each other? And who owns each one — a central team, or individual engineers?

Interviewer

Per pipeline, daily. Call it 10 revenue-critical, 50 business-critical, 140 purely analytical. Ownership is spread across individual engineers.

Candidate

That split is the first real design decision. And since ownership is spread out, the central team's real job is making it hard to ship a badly-behaved pipeline in the first place.

Three tiers: highest — 10 pipelines, 1-hour limit, pages immediately, requires idempotent tasks, concurrency capped at one, tested rerun runbook before it ships. Middle — 50 pipelines, 4-hour limit, pages during hours, email overnight. Lowest — 140 pipelines, 24-hour limit, chat notification only. I'd enforce these automatically — a highest-tier pipeline that fails an idempotency check should simply fail to ship.

Interviewer

What breaks first at 200 pipelines?

Candidate

The scheduler itself — slows down around several hundred simultaneous tasks, worst at burst moments like midnight UTC. I'd run it in a high-availability setup with an autoscaling worker pool. Second, the polling problem — if 100 pipelines all check upstream every minute, that's real load. I'd make sure everything releases its worker slot between checks. Third, tier inheritance — a business-critical pipeline depending on a purely analytical one inherits its looser guarantee whether it wants to or not. I'd block a pipeline from claiming a tighter guarantee than the loosest thing it depends on.

Interviewer

What's the plan if the scheduler itself goes down?

Candidate

High availability handles most of it — runs pause, nothing new queues, nothing corrupts. (pauses) Let me push on that — "no corruption, just stalled work" is only true if catch-up afterward is controlled. If the scheduler's down six hours and catch-up kicks in with the default permissive concurrency, we're back in the war-story failure mode. So: high availability, plus a concurrency cap of one on anything writing to shared data, plus not blindly auto-replaying every missed schedule. Drop any one of those and an outage turns into a real data incident.

Common Mistakes People Make

  1. Treating the dependency diagram as the whole design. Concurrency limits, retry policy, and idempotency are where the real design work is.
  2. Leaving the concurrency cap at its default for any pipeline writing to a shared data slice — the classic real-world incident.
  3. Using a polling sensor without releasing the worker slot between checks. Fine with a handful of pipelines, falls over at scale.
  4. Turning on retries before the task is idempotent. Data corrupts silently.
  5. One reliability promise across every pipeline. Direct path to on-call burnout — tiered promises are the fix.

The One-Sentence Version

Orchestration is a contract about failure behavior, not just a schedule.

Four Operational Building Blocks

  • Idempotency — replace, insert-or-update, or atomic swap
  • Retries — a few attempts, longer waits each time
  • Concurrency limits — cap same-pipeline runs to 1 for shared data
  • Sensors — release the slot between checks, or use notify-instead

Recovery Time Budget

budget = detect + diagnose + rerun + buffer

Backfill Options, Fastest to Slowest

  1. Rerun just the broken piece (needs idempotency)
  2. Full rebuild from scratch
  3. Manual fix (emergency only)

Reliability Tiers

  • Highest — ~1h tolerance, pages immediately
  • High — ~4h tolerance, pages during hours
  • Lower — ~24h tolerance, chat only

Three Lines Worth Memorizing

  • "Orchestration is a contract, not scheduling."
  • "Idempotency plus bounded concurrency. One without the other doesn't save you."
  • "The rerun command lives in the runbook before the pipeline ships."
A Couple of Extra Ideas — from the older 2025 edition
  • Fan-out, fan-in coordination: one dataset branches into many downstream transformations, which later collect back together. A fan-in step's slowest upstream piece controls overall latency, and one bad upstream file can block everything downstream.
  • Event-driven vs. schedule-driven: schedules are simple but can waste time waiting for a fixed clock time; event triggers reduce latency but add coordination complexity. Most mature platforms use both.
  • Retries can have real business consequences: in financial or healthcare contexts, blindly retrying a task with an external side effect (like a payment) can trigger it twice. Sometimes the right move is "mark incomplete, needs manual review" instead of an automatic retry.
